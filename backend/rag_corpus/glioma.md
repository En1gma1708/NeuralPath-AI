# Gliomas — Clinical Overview

## Epidemiology

There are approximately 6 cases of gliomas diagnosed per 100,000 individuals
every year in the United States. Among the roughly 80,000-90,000 newly
diagnosed primary brain tumors annually, roughly 25% are gliomas.
Glioblastomas specifically account for about 12,000 cases yearly,
representing approximately 15% of all newly diagnosed brain tumors and 50%
of malignant cases.

## Clinical Presentation

Common symptoms include headaches, seizures, nausea, vomiting, and focal
neurological deficits. Physical examination findings may reveal focal
weakness, sensory loss, or altered consciousness from mass effect and edema.
Approximately 3-10% of low-grade lesions are discovered incidentally during
unrelated investigations.

## Imaging Characteristics

MRI serves as the primary diagnostic tool for gliomas. Low-grade tumors
typically lack enhancement, while high-grade variants demonstrate variable
enhancement patterns. Gliomas commonly show strong peripheral enhancement
around a necrotic core with a pronounced mass effect, evidenced by
significant midline shift in more aggressive cases. MRI effectively
evaluates cerebral edema and tumor characteristics. Additional imaging
includes head CT for acute findings, chest/abdomen/pelvis CT for metastatic
workup, spinal MRI for ependymomas, and diffusion tensor imaging for
surgical planning near eloquent cortex.

## Grading and Classification

The 2021 WHO classification incorporates molecular markers (IDH mutations
and 1p/19q co-deletions) alongside histopathology. Astrocytomas are
classified by IDH status; oligodendrogliomas require both IDH mutation and
1p/19q co-deletion; glioblastomas are IDH-wildtype only.

## Management

Standard treatment involves maximal safe resection combined with
chemoradiation. The Stupp protocol represents standard care for high-grade
tumors: 60 Gy radiotherapy over 6 weeks with concurrent temozolomide.
Low-grade lesions may require only surgical resection with monitoring.
