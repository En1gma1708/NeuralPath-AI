# Pituitary Adenoma — Clinical Overview

## Epidemiology

Pituitary adenomas are remarkably common, with prevalence varying by
detection method. A meta-analysis revealed an average frequency of
pituitary adenomas of 16.7% across studies, though autopsy findings showed
14.4% versus 22.5% on radiological imaging. Clinically relevant adenomas
(those causing symptoms or hormonal effects) occur at significantly lower
rates — approximately 89.1 per 100,000 persons.

The most frequent subtypes include prolactinomas (50% of cases),
nonfunctioning adenomas (30%), somatotroph adenomas (11%), and corticotroph
adenomas (5%). Sex distribution varies by type; prolactinomas
predominantly affect women of reproductive age, while acromegaly is more
common in men.

## Functioning vs. Nonfunctioning Presentation

Functioning adenomas produce excess hormones causing characteristic
syndromes: prolactinomas cause infertility, amenorrhea, galactorrhea, and
erectile dysfunction; growth hormone-secreting adenomas (acromegaly) cause
coarse features, prognathism, arthritis, and carpal tunnel syndrome;
ACTH-secreting adenomas (Cushing disease) cause weight gain, muscle
weakness, moon facies, and purple striae; TSH-secreting adenomas cause
palpitations, weight loss, tremor, and goiter.

Nonfunctioning adenomas cause symptoms through mass effect rather than
hormone secretion, producing visual deficits and hormonal deficiencies from
compression.

## Imaging Characteristics

MRI represents the gold standard for evaluation. Adenomas are classified by
size: microadenomas (<10 mm), often incidental findings, usually
asymptomatic; macroadenomas (≥10 mm), more likely to cause compressive
symptoms; giant adenomas (>40 mm). Gadolinium-enhanced coronal and sagittal
T1-weighted images differentiate adenomas from other sellar lesions. Visual
complications occur in approximately 40% to 60% of patients with
suprasellar extension compressing the optic chiasm, typically causing
bitemporal hemianopia.

## Management

Treatment depends on tumor type and functional status. Nonfunctional
macroadenomas with visual deficits, optic chiasm compression, or
progressive growth warrant transsphenoidal resection. Radiotherapy
addresses residual or recurrent disease.

Prolactinomas respond well to dopamine agonists (cabergoline efficacy
>90%), with surgery reserved for medical resistance or pregnancy planning.

GH-secreting adenomas require transsphenoidal surgery as first-line
treatment, achieving IGF-1 normalization in 80-90% of microadenomas and
40-60% of macroadenomas. Somatostatin analogs manage persistent elevation.

ACTH-secreting adenomas demand urgent surgical intervention, achieving
remission (cortisol <2 μg/dL) in 70-90% of cases, with medical therapy
supporting persistent or recurrent disease.
