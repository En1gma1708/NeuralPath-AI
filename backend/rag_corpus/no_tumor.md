# Understanding a Normal Brain MRI Report

## Overall Purpose

A radiologist interprets brain MRI images and writes a report for the
referring healthcare provider. The report may contain medical terminology,
but patients have the right to access and understand their results.
Questions about a report should be discussed directly with a physician.

## Key Report Sections

**Findings section** — describes what the radiologist observed during the
scan, including the significant absence of concerning features. A
radiologist may use terms like "normal," "unremarkable," or "normal for
patient age." Many things radiologists see on imaging are quite common and
cause no harm.

**Impression section** — the most critical part of the report; it
summarizes the conclusions and recommendations the radiologist wants the
referring provider to be aware of.

## What a Normal/Clear Result Looks Like

When a brain MRI is normal, the report may state findings such as "there is
no evidence of an intracranial mass, bleed, or infarct" or "there is no
abnormal parenchymal or meningeal enhancement." Related phrasing includes
"normal brain MRI," "no acute intracranial abnormality," or "unremarkable
brain MRI."

"No acute intracranial abnormality" specifically means there is no evidence
of an urgent problem inside the skull — no recent stroke, hemorrhage, mass
effect, or other acute finding. It does not rule out chronic changes,
incidental findings, or gradually progressive disease; clinicians integrate
this statement with clinical exam findings, risk factors, and known
age-related MRI changes.

## Common Normal or Incidental Findings

Findings that shouldn't cause alarm on their own include mild brain volume
loss (atrophy), age-related white matter changes, sinus mucosal thickening,
and small cysts — many of these are normal, age-appropriate variations
rather than pathology. A "clear" scan report should still be reviewed with
a physician who can integrate it with the full clinical picture, rather
than interpreted in isolation.
